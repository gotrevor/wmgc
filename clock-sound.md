# Why chess clocks are not allowed to beep

*US Chess rule 16B2a, and the two rules that explain it*

[ Claude wrote all of this.  I think it's good stuff - but, treat with care!  -Trevor ]

---

## The rule

A tournament chess clock is required to be silent, and the ban is explicit that it covers a low-time warning, not just the flag itself:

> Digital clocks must be set to produce no sound.  This includes, but is not limited to: beeping when a player has run out of time in the final time control; beeping when a player reaches the end of any time control; **warning beeps to indicate a player is approaching the end of a time control**; and beeping when a player presses the clock.  A digital clock that can be set to produce no sound is preferable to a clock that cannot.

**US Chess, Official Rules of Chess, 7th ed., rule 16B2a (Sound)**

## Why: a fallen flag is a claim, not a fact

Under US Chess rules, running out of time does not end a game.  A player has to notice and claim it, and nobody else is permitted to help:

> Only the players in a game may call attention to the fall of a flag; it is considered to have fallen only when either player points this out.  A director must never initiate a time-forfeit claim.  Spectators, including players of other games, who point out the fall of a flag in any manner, may be disciplined by the tournament director to the point of expulsion from the playing room, loss of their own games, or expulsion from the tournament.

**rule 13C1 (Only players may call flag)**

> No one, except a player's opponent, may call that player's attention to the fact that a flag is down, the opponent has moved, or the player has forgotten to press the clock after moving.  These prohibitions also apply to the director.

**rule 16Y (Assisting players with time management prohibited)**

That is the whole argument.  A beeping clock announces the flag to the room, which is precisely what a spectator can be expelled for doing and what the director is forbidden to do.  A warning beep is worse: it is time-management assistance, delivered before the flag, to the player losing the race.  The rule is about keeping the clock from making the claim.

## The added bonus: a much quieter playing area

Noise is not what the rule is aimed at, but it is a real benefit and worth saying out loud.  A hall of silent clocks is a *much* quieter room than one where several dozen machines are chirping through everybody's endgame - and in a long tournament that quiet is worth something on its own, whatever the rulebook was thinking about.

## FIDE decided the opposite way

The same rulebook notes the contrast in a TD Tip: *"Remember in FIDE competitions the arbiter can call the flag, and should do so."*  Once an official is the one who calls time, a clock that announces it is redundant rather than illegal, and FIDE's equipment regulation asks only that an audible alarm have a setting to switch it off.

## And in Go

Go inherited the opposite convention.  Byo-yomi has been counted aloud by a timekeeper for as long as it has existed, so an audible clock is idiomatic rather than suspect, and Go clocks ship a low-time warning as a feature.  Same hardware, opposite answers about whether the equipment may tell you what it knows.

Which leaves a live question for our own events, and it is the reason this page exists: **should the clocks in a Go tournament warn you before your main time runs out?**  There is a real case on each side - the help of a warning against the quiet of a silent hall - and it is on the New England Open player survey.
